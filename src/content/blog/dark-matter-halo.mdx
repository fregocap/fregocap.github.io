### Glossary and prerequisites

*Assumes classical mechanics (action-angle variables) and introductory statistical
mechanics. The astrophysics terms used below:*

- **Distribution function $f(E)$** - the density of particles in phase space
  (position-velocity space). For an isotropic system it depends only on energy $E$.
  The real-space density is its velocity integral, $\rho(r)=\int f\, \mathrm d^3v$.
- **Phase space** - the 6D space of positions and velocities $(\mathbf r, \mathbf v)$.
  Liouville's theorem: $f$ is conserved along orbits, so it is invariant under
  slow (adiabatic) changes of the potential.
- **Isotropic velocity distribution** - velocities have no preferred direction;
  equivalently a full, unbiased spread of orbital eccentricities. A *Maxwellian*
  is the thermal (Gaussian-in-velocity) isotropic case.
- **Virialised halo** - a self-gravitating system in equilibrium, obeying the
  virial theorem $2K+W=0$; its particles have random (not ordered) velocities.
- **Adiabatic invariant** - a phase-space integral ($L$, $J_r$) conserved when
  the potential changes slowly compared to the orbital period.
- **Cusp / spike** - a power-law central density rise, $\rho\propto r^{-\gamma}$.
  "Spike" usually denotes the steep enhancement around a compact object.
- **Loss cone** - the region of the orbit-space whose pericentres reach the central
  object; such orbits are captured and removed.
- **Collisional relaxation** - slow evolution of orbits from cumulative two-body
  gravitational encounters, characterized by a *relaxation time* $t_\mathrm{relax}$.
- **Fokker-Planck / detailed balance** - the diffusion equation governing $f$ under
  relaxation; its stationary state is fixed by the balance of drift and diffusion
  (fluctuation-dissipation).
- **Dynamical friction** - the drag a massive body feels moving through a background
  of lighter particles (here, DM), which it gravitationally focuses into a trailing
  wake.
- **EMRI** - extreme-mass-ratio inspiral: a compact object spiralling into a much
  larger black hole, a key gravitational-wave source for LISA.

## 0. The question

A star forms inside a dark-matter (DM) halo. Initially the DM is spread out in an
approximately **uniform** density profile, $\rho_\mathrm{DM}\simeq\text{const}$.
As the baryonic gas collapses to form the star, it deepens the gravitational
potential at the centre. Dark matter feels **only gravity** - no cooling, no
radiation, no collisions - so it responds by contracting too.

The questions we answer here, from first principles:

1. What is the final DM density profile ? (It is a power law, $\rho\propto r^{-\gamma}$.)
2. What is the exponent $\gamma$?
3. Why do two standard methods give **two different answers** - $\gamma=9/4$ and $\gamma=3/2$?
4. What survives a supernova, and what survives billions of years of stellar scattering?
5. What does all this say about whether observed DM spikes imply *primordial* black holes?

The punchline for (2)-(3): the answer depends entirely on **what orbits the DM particles
are on**. Circular orbits give $\gamma=9/4$; a realistic isotropic (Maxwellian) velocity
distribution gives $\gamma=3/2$. The difference is a lesson in why "adiabatic contraction"
is not a single number.

---

## 1. The tool: adiabatic invariants

When a system's potential changes **slowly** compared to the orbital period of a particle
(the *adiabatic* regime), certain phase-space integrals are conserved. For a particle in
a spherical potential the two relevant **adiabatic invariants** are:


$$
L = |\,\mathbf r \times \mathbf v\,| \qquad\text{(angular momentum)}
$$

$$
J_r = \frac{1}{2 \pi} \oint p_r\, \mathrm dr = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{r_-}^{r_+}\!\sqrt{2\big(E-\Phi(r)\big)-\frac{L^2}{r^2}}\;\mathrm dr \qquad\text{(radial action)}
$$

where $r_\pm$ are the peri/apocentre. The physical content of "adiabatic" is:
**as the potential $\Phi$ morphs from initial to final, each particle keeps its
$(J_r, L)$ fixed**, and this determines its new orbit.

Two limits use these differently, and that is the entire origin of the $9/4$-vs-$3/2$
discrepancy.

---

## 2. The circular-orbit model (Blumenthal et al. 1986)

The simplest and oldest prescription assumes every DM particle is on a **circular orbit**.
A circular orbit has $J_r=0$; it is fully specified by $L$ alone. Conservation
of $L$ for a circular orbit is equivalent to the statement that the quantity

$$
M(<r)\,r = \text{const}
$$

is conserved for each shell, where $M(<r)$ is the total enclosed mass. This is the
**Blumenthal invariant**. Concretely: label a DM shell by its initial radius $r_i$; after
the baryons collapse to a central mass, its final radius $r_f$ satisfies

$$
\big[M_\mathrm{DM}(<r_f)+M_\mathrm{bar}(<r_f)\big]\,r_f \;=\; M_\mathrm{tot, init}(<r_i)\,r_i.
$$

### Derivation of the slope

Start from a uniform sphere, $\rho_i=\text{const}$, so $M_\mathrm{tot, init}(<r_i)\propto r_i^3$.
For inner shells the collapsed baryons dominate and act like a point mass $M_\star$, so
$M_\mathrm{bar}(<r_f)\approx M_\star$. Then,

$$
M_\star\, r_f \;\propto\; r_i^3\cdot r_i = r_i^4 \quad \Longrightarrow\quad r_f \propto r_i^4 .
$$

Mass conservation shell-by-shell, $\rho_f\,r_f^2\,\mathrm dr_f = \rho_i\,r_i^2\,\mathrm dr_i$
with $\rho_i=\text{const}$ and $\mathrm dr_f\propto r_i^3\, \mathrm dr_i$, gives

$$
\rho_f \;\propto\; \frac{r_i^2}{r_i^4\cdot r_i^3}\cdot r_i^2
\;=\; r_i^{-9} \;\propto\; \big(r_f^{1/4}\big)^{-9} \;=\; r_f^{-9/4}.
$$

$$
\boxed{\;\gamma_\mathrm{circ} = \tfrac{9}{4} = 2.25 \;}
$$

More generally, for an initial cusp $\rho_i \propto r^{-\alpha}$ the circular model
gives $\gamma = (9-2\alpha)/(4-\alpha)$, which is $9/4$ for $\alpha$ and rises slowly to
$7/3$ for $\alpha=1$.

The figure below shows a numerical implementation: a uniform profile is contracted through
successive stages and the inner slope locks onto $\approx 2.2$-$2.3$.

** But circular orbits are a fiction.** Real DM in a virialised halo has an isotropic
velocity distribution - a full spread of eccentricities. That changes the answer.

## 3. The Liouville / phase-space model (Young 1980)

Now we do it honestly. The DM starts with an **isotropic Maxwellian** velocity distribution
inside the uniform sphere. We conserve the *full* adiabatic invariants $(J_r, L)$, not just
$L$.

### The initial potential is harmonic

A uniform sphere of total density $\rho_0$ produces, inside itself, a **harmonic**
potential:

$$
\Phi_i(r) = \Phi_c + \tfrac12\,\omega_0^2\, r^2, \qquad \omega_0^2 = \frac{4\pi G \rho_0}{3}=\frac{GM_\mathrm{tot}}{R^3}.
$$

For the harmonic oscillator the radial action has a **closed form**:

$$
J_r^{\rm HO} = \frac{E-\Phi_c}{\omega_0} -L.
$$

### The final potential is Keplerian

After the baryons collapse to a point mass $M_\star$, an inner DM particle sees a
**Keplerian** potential $\Phi_f=-GM_\star/r$, whose radial action is also closed-form

$$
J_r^{\rm Kep} = \frac{GM_\star}{\sqrt{2|E_f|}} - L.
$$

### Equate the invariants - and $L$ cancels

Setting $J_r^{\rm HO} = J_r^{\rm Kep}$ (and using that $L$ is separately conserved,
so the $-L$ on both sides cancels):

$$
\frac{E_i - \Phi_c}{\omega_0} = \frac{GM_\star}{\sqrt{2|E_f|}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{\;|E_f| = \frac{(GM_\star\,\omega_0)^2}{2\, \varepsilon_i^2}\;}, \qquad
\varepsilon_i \equiv E_i - \Phi_c = \tfrac12 v^2 + \tfrac12\omega_0^2 r^2 .
$$

This is an **exact, analytic map** from a particle's initial energy to its final
Keplerian orbit - no time integration required. The final semi-major axis is
$a_f= GM_\star/2|E_f|$ and the eccentricity follows from $L$:
$e_f^2 = 1 - (L\omega_0/\varepsilon_i)^2$.

### Why the density goes as $r^{-3/2}$

Here is the crucial kinematic fact. In a Keplerian potential a particle at small
radius moves fast: $v_r(r)\sim\sqrt{2GM_\star/r}\propto r^{-1/2}$. The **time it spends**
near radius $r$ is $\propto 1/v_r \propto r^{1/2}$. The time-averaged density contribution
of *any* orbit passing through $r$ is therefore

$$
\rho(r) \;\propto\; \frac{1}{r^2\,v_r(r)} \;\propto\; \frac{r^{1/2}}{r^2} \;=\; r^{-3/2}.
$$

This $r^{-3/2}$ is **universal**: it does not depend on the orbit's $a$ or $e$, only on
the Keplerian potential. Summed over the population of orbits, the inner density is

$$
\boxed{\;\rho_f(r)\propto r^{-3/2},\qquad \gamma_\mathrm{Liouville}=\tfrac32=1.5\;}
$$

This is the result of **Young (1980)** and, in the DM-during-star-formation context,
**Capela, Pshirkov & Tinyakov (2014)** (arXiv:1403.7098).

### Numerical confirmation

In order to get a numerical confirmation, we have created a script that samples $3\times10^5$
particles from a uniform Maxwellian sphere, applies the exact invariant map, and reconstructs
the density by solving Kepler's equation (sampling mean anomaly uniformly correctly weights
positions by time spent). The measured slope is $\gamma = 1.500$ to three digits. Note the
median eccentricity of the final orbits is $e\approx 0.67$ - the orbits are **highly eccentric**,
which is the whole point.

## 4. Why $9/4$ and $3/2$ differ - the physical heart of it

The two models conserve **different things** because they assume different orbits:

| | Circular (Blumenthal) | Eccentric / isotropic (Liouville) |
|---|---|---|
| Conserved | $L$ only ($J_r=0$) | $L$ **and** $J_r$ |
| Each particle occupies | a fixed radius | a *range* $[r_-, r_+]$ |
| Density at $r$ built from | the one shell mapped to $r$ | time-averaging of many orbits |
| Result | $\gamma = 9/4$ | $\gamma = 3/2$ |

**The one-sentence version**: the circular model pins each particle to a *radius*;
the Liouville model puts each particle on an *orbit*, and an eccentric particle spends
most of its time near **apocenter** - far from the center - which dilutes the inner
density and makes the cusp shallower.

**Interpolation.** If you allowed only orbits up to some maximum eccentricity
$e_\mathrm{max}$, you would interpolate: $e_\mathrm{max}\to0$ recovers $9/4$,
$e_\mathrm{max}\to1$ (full isotropic) gives $3/2$. The value $3/2$ is essentially a
kinematic identity of the Keplerian potential (the $r^{-1/2}$ velocity law), not
a coincidence - which is why, as we will see, it is remarkably robust.

### A cleaner bookkeeping: slope ↔ distribution function

For an isotropic population in a Keplerian potential with distribution function
$f(E)\propto |E|^p$, a short calculation
($\rho(r)=\int f\,\mathrm d^3v \propto \int_0^{v_\mathrm{esc}} (\,\phi-\tfrac12 v^2)^p v^2\,\mathrm dv \propto \phi^{p+3/2}$, with $\phi=GM_\star/r$) gives

$$
\boxed{\;\gamma = p + \tfrac32\;}
$$

So $\gamma=3/2 \Leftrightarrow p=0$ (a **flat** DF in energy - the Young spike); $\gamma=9/4 \Leftrightarrow p=3/4$. We will reuse this identity constantly below.

## 5. General-relativistic corrections

The Newtonian $r^{-3/2}$ spike would run to $r\to0$. GR modifies the innermost region:

- **Loss-cone cutoff (dominant).** Any DM particle whose pericentre falls inside
  the innermost stable circular orbit is captured. For Schwarzschild,
  $r_\mathrm{ISCO}=6GM_\star/c^2$. The spike is simply truncated below
  $\sim r_\mathrm{ISCO}$: $\rho\approx0$ there, $r^{-3/2}$ outside.
- **Black-hole spin.** For a maximally spinning Kerr hole,
  $r_\mathrm{ISCO}\to GM_\star/c^2$, six times smaller. Since $\rho\propto r^{-3/2}$,
  the density at the inner edge is enhanced by $\sim6^{3/2}\approx15$ - a large factor for
  annihilation or gravitational-wave signals.
- **The asymptotic slope is unchanged.** The GR correction to the effective potential enters
  at $\mathcal O(r_s/r)$, so $\gamma(r)=\tfrac32+\mathcal O(r_s/r)$; in the observable
  region $r\gg r_s$ the slope $3/2$.

The GR-consistent Schwarzschild treatment (Sadeghian, Ferrer & Will 2013) confirms
the slope is preserved outside the loss cone with a modest inner-normalisation change.


## 6. What a supernova does: the slope is invariant

Most stars that leave a compact remnant explode first, ejecting a mass fraction
$f_\mathrm{ej}$ **impulsively** - fast compared to any DM orbital period. In the
impulsive limit the DM positions are frozen while the potential suddenly shallows.
A particle at position $r_\mathrm{SN}$ at the instant of explosion get an energy kick

$$
E \;\to\; E' = E + \frac{G\,f_\mathrm{ej}M_\star}{r_\mathrm{SN}}.
$$

It escapes if $E'>0$. Writing $E=-GM_\star/2a_f$, the **survival condition** is

$$
r_\mathrm{SN} > 2\,a_f\, f_\mathrm{ej}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
1-e_f\cos\eta > 2 f_\mathrm{ej},
$$

where $\eta$ is the orbital phase. **This condition is scale-free** - it depends
only on the orbital phase and eccentricity, not on the absolute orbit size $a_f$.
Because the eccentricity distribution is the same at every radial scale, the SN removes
the **same fraction of particles at every radius**. Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\;\gamma_\mathrm{remnant} = \gamma_\mathrm{formation}\quad\text{for any }f_\mathrm{ej}\;}
$$

Only the **normalisation** drops (down to $\sim$8% survival for a neutron-star-forming
$f_\mathrm{ej}=0.9$). The classic Blaauw (1961) result - a circular orbit unbinds
at $f_\mathrm{ej}>1/2$ - is recovered as the $e\to0$ edge case, but eccentric orbits
caught near apocenter survive even $f_\mathrm{ej}>1/2$.

I have created a script to confirm this: the remnant slope stays pinned at $1.5$ across
the entire range $f_\mathrm{ej}\in[0,0.95]$, while the amplitude falls.

Keplerian-to-Keplerian mass growth rescales every orbit by a common factor and
leaves the slope untouched. **Neither impulsive mass loss nor subsequent accretion
can move $\gamma$ away from its formation value in the Keplerian regime.**

## 7. Billions of years later: stellar scattering and the Bahcall-Wolf attractor

Over cosmic time the DM cusp is stirred by **two-body gravitational encounters**
with stars - collisional relaxation. Bahcall & Wolf (1976, ApJ 209, 214) solved the
energy-space Fokker-Planck equation for stars around a massive black hole and found
a **steady-state attractor**: a constant-flux cusp

$$
f(E)\propto|E|^{1/4},\qquad \rho\propto r^{-7/4},
$$

independent of initial conditions. In our $\gamma=p+3/2$ language this is $p=1/4$.
The exponent $7/4$ is the unique power law for which the inward energy flux is
radius-independent - a genuine dynamical fixed point.

Their 1977 sequel (ApJ 216, 883) and Alexander & Hopman (2009) treat **multiple
masses**: heavy species keep $\gamma_H=7/4$, while a **light** species (like DM,
much lighter than stars) relaxes to a shallower $\gamma\to 3/2$. So:

| Regime | What scatters the DM | Attractor $\gamma_\mathrm{eq}$ | $p_\mathrm{eq}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| DM self-relaxation (single mass) | DM-DM | $7/4 = 1.75$ | $1/4$ |
| DM as light species | heavier stars | $\to 3/2 = 1.50$ | $0$ |

### Emulating relaxation

Rather than solve the full Fokker-Planck PDE, we have emulated relaxation with a
stochastic (Langevin) process **engineered to have the correct stationary
distribution**. Working in binding energy $x=-E$, we use the fluctuation-dissipation
relation $A=D\,(\ln\pi)' + D'$ to pick drift $A$ and diffusion $D$ so the equilibrium
is exactly the target attractor $\pi(x)\propto x^{\,p_\mathrm{eq}-5/2}$. Choosing
$D\propto x^2$ makes the process a geometric Brownian motion in $\ln x$ - every
radius relaxes on one timescale - integrated exactly in log-space:

$$
\mathrm d (\ln x) = \mu\,\mathrm dt + \sigma\,\mathrm dW,\qquad \mu = p_\mathrm{eq}-\tfrac32,\quad \sigma=\sqrt2 .
$$

Reflecting boundaries represent resupply by the outer halo and the inner loss cone.
The result: **both** formation slopes converge to the attractor, erasing the memory
of the initial condition.

The physically important corollary: because DM is much lighter than stars, the
relevant attractor is the **mass-segregation** one, $\gamma\to 3/2$ - the *same*
value as the formation slope. **Relaxation of DM by stars reinforces $\gamma=1.5$;
it does not lift it.**

## 8. The payoff: do observed spikes imply *primordial* black holes ?

Several black-hole low-mass X-ray binaries (A0620-00, XTE J1118+480, Nova Muscae
1991) show anomalous orbital-period decay that has been attributed to dynamical
friction against a **DM spike**. Because a light black hole formed by stellar
collapse is "not expected" to carry a spike, some authors (e.g. arXiv:2406.07624)
argue the primaries could be **primordial** black holes, which form dense
secondary-infall spikes with $\gamma\simeq 9/4$

Two things sharpen this:

1. **How steep must the observed spike be?** An N-body analysis *with feedback*
   (arXiv:2510.11635) finds the friction requires
   $$\gamma \gtrsim 2.15\text{–}2.2\ \ (\text{A0620-00, XTE J1118+480}),\qquad \gamma\gtrsim2.3\ \ (\text{Nova Muscae}).$$

2. **How steep can a *stellar-origin* spike be?** Everything above gives a hard ceiling:
   - Formation (Young/Liouville): $\gamma = 3/2$.
   - Supernova: preserves it exactly.
   - Fallback accretion: preserves it (Keplerian rescaling).
   - Relaxation: mass-segregation drives DM toward $3/2$; self-relaxation caps at $7/4$.
   $$\boxed{\;\gamma_\mathrm{stellar}\ \le\ 7/4 = 1.75\;}$$


### The verdict

There is a **clean gap**: a stellar-origin black hole cannot exceed $\gamma\simeq1.75$,
while the observation require $\gamma\gtrsim2.2$, which only the **primordial**
secondary-infall channel ($\gamma=9/4$) naturally reaches. So the quantitative
calculation **supports** the primordial interpretation - but it replaces the hand-wave
("hard to see how a spike survives") in the literature with an actual number and an
actual mechanism-by-mechanism argument.

**Honest caveats.** (i) This confirms, rather than overturns, the prevailing
assumption. (ii) The whole edifice is contingent on the period decays being due
to DM at all, which is contested. (iii) The relaxation ceiling should be nailed
with real Alexander-Hopman coefficients, not the toy. (iv) One genuinely open
effect could change the picture: the **natal kick**.

---

## 9. The open problem: natal kicks

A supernova imparts a **kick velocity** to the newborn compact object (from
asymmetric ejecta and neutrino emission), typically tens to hundreds of km/s. This
moves the black hole **out of the center of its own DM spike**. Unlike the
symmetric mass loss of §6 - which we showed preserves the slope - a kick
displaces the potential minimum relative to the frozen DM, and could:

- strip the spike almost entirely if the kick exceeds the DM orbital velocities, or
- leave a lopsided remnant cusp that the black hole slowly re-captures.

Neither the primordial-origin papers nor the pipeline here has modelled it, and it
is the one ingredient that could move $\gamma_\mathrm{stellar}$ or destroy the spike
outright. **It is the natural next calculation** - and the impulsive machinery of
§6 extends to it directly: add a common velocity boost $\mathbf v_\mathrm{kick}$ to
every particle's frame at the SN instant and re-evaluate which orbits stay bound.

## 10. Summary

| Stage | Mechanism | Effect on slope $\gamma$ |
|---|---|---|
| Formation (circular approx.) | Blumenthal $M r=$const | $9/4 = 2.25$ |
| Formation (isotropic, correct) | Liouville / Young 1980 | $3/2 = 1.50$ |
| Supernova (mass loss) | impulsive, scale-free | **unchanged** |
| Fallback accretion | Keplerian rescaling | **unchanged** |
| Relaxation (DM by stars) | mass-segregation | $\to 3/2$ |
| Relaxation (DM self) | Bahcall-Wolf | $\to 7/4$ |
| **Stellar-origin ceiling** | - | $\le 7/4 = 1.75$ |
| Observations require | dynamical friction (N-body) | $\gtrsim 2.2$ |
| Primordial (secondary infall) | Bertschinger 1985 | $9/4 = 2.25$ |

The DM density profile built during star formation is a **power law** because
the final potential is Keplerian; its exponent is **$3/2$, not $9/4$**, because real
DM is on eccentric orbits and spends its time near apocentre; that exponent is
**robust** against supernovae, accretion, and stellar relaxation; and the gap
between the stellar ceiling ($\le1.75$) and the observational requirement
($\gtrsim2.2$) is what makes DM spikes around X-ray binaries a genuine probe of
primordial black holes.
